# Foreword

You have secured a job offer in China. Your employer has told you they will "handle the visa." Your friends have told you to "just get a work permit." Neither of these statements is sufficient preparation for what you are about to navigate.

This is not a generic overview of China's work culture. This is the step-by-step operational manual that covers: what documents you must obtain before you leave home, how to avoid having your residence permit rejected at the immigration desk, whether your employer is legally required to pay your social insurance, what happens if you are fired or want to quit, and how to enforce your legal rights when things go wrong.

This guide draws from official Chinese government regulations on work permits and residence permits, actual legal case outcomes from Chinese labor arbitration tribunals and courts across multiple provinces, and the current operational procedures of Beijing's work permit processing authorities. Every recommendation in this guide is grounded in enforceable legal requirements, not hearsay.


# Part I: The Legal Framework – What You Are Entering


## 1.1 The Three-Document System

Working legally in China requires three separate but interdependent documents:


| Document | Issued By | Purpose | Validity |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Work Permit (外国人工作许可证) | Ministry of Science and Technology / local bureaus | Authorizes you to work for a specific employer in a specific role | Tied to employment contract; up to 5 years for A-class talent |
| Work-Class Residence Permit (工作类居留许可) | Exit & Entry Administration (public security bureau) | Authorizes your physical stay in China for work purposes | Up to 5 years; must be renewed before expiry |
| Z-Visa (Z字签证) | Chinese embassy/consulate abroad | Entry permit to travel to China and activate the above | Single-entry; valid 30 days after arrival |

Critical rule: You cannot work in China with only a Z-visa. The Z-visa is a transit document. Within 30 days of arrival, you must apply for the Work-Class Residence Permit at the local Exit & Entry Administration. Failure to do so results in overstay fines (RMB 500 per day) and potential visa cancellation.


## 1.2 The Three Talent Categories (A, B, C)

China classifies foreign workers into three tiers, which determine processing speed, document requirements, and renewal terms:

Category A (High-End Talent): No age, education, or work experience restrictions. Certain document requirements may be replaced by a personal commitment letter (承诺制).

Category B (Professional Talent): Must be under Chinese statutory retirement age (60 for men, 55 for women). Requires degree, work experience (2+ years typically), and complete documentation.

Category C (Other/Unskilled): Strict quotas; rarely issued to new foreign workers outside specific sectors (e.g., performing arts, short-term technical assignments).

Your employer determines your category based on salary, qualifications, and the role. If your salary is below RMB 400,000 annually, you are almost certainly Category B and must provide full documentation.


# Part II: Pre-Departure – What You Must Prepare Before Leaving Home


## 2.1 Document Authentication – The New Apostille Rule (Critical)

As of November 7, 2023, China acceded to the Hague Apostille Convention. This dramatically simplified document legalization.

Before November 2023: You needed your documents notarized, then authenticated by your country's foreign ministry, then legalized by the Chinese embassy/consulate — a process taking 4 weeks.

From November 2023 onward: You only need:

Your original document (degree, police clearance, etc.)

Apostille from the competent authority in your country (e.g., U.S. Department of State, UK Foreign Office)

A certified Chinese translation

This cuts processing time to 1 weeks.


## 2.2 Documents You Must Obtain Before Boarding the Plane

Based on the official work permit application requirements for Beijing, you need the following original authenticated documents before you depart:


| Document | Requirement Details | Time to Obtain |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Highest Degree Certificate | Must be Apostilled. If issued outside China, must have Apostille from the issuing country's competent authority | 1 weeks with Apostille |
| Police Clearance Certificate | Must be issued within 6 months of application. Must show no criminal record. Must be Apostilled. Warning: Sworn/declaratory statements of no criminal record are NOT accepted | 2 weeks (country dependent) |
| Medical Examination Report | Must be issued by a Chinese-designated medical institution after arrival, OR a physical examination record verified by Chinese health authorities | Obtain after arrival (see Section 3.3) |
| Employment Contract | Must be in Chinese. Must include: work location, job description, compensation, start date. Must be signed by you and stamped by employer | Negotiate before departure |
| Work Experience Verification | Letters from previous employers detailing position, duration, and projects. Must include contact details for verification | 2 weeks |

Critical note on police clearance: If you have lived outside your home country for more than one continuous year, you must obtain a police clearance from that country as well. "Frequent traveler" status does not exempt you.


## 2.3 The Z-Visa Application

Before your embassy appointment, your employer must obtain Work Permit Notification (工作许可通知) online through the Chinese work permit system. This electronic document is the basis for your Z-visa application.

Documents for the Z-visa:

Work Permit Notification (printed)

Valid passport (minimum 6 months validity)

Visa application form

Passport-size photos (Chinese specifications — usually 33mm x 48mm)

Invitation letter from employer

Timeline: Work permit notification takes 5 working days from submission. Visa processing takes 3 working days depending on your country.


# Part III: Arrival – The First 30 Days (Critical Window)

The first 30 days after arrival are the most operationally intense period of your move. Missing deadlines during this window creates cascading legal and practical problems.


## 3.1 Day 1: Accommodation Registration (24-Hour Rule)

Legal requirement: All foreign nationals must register their accommodation within 24 hours of arrival.

Hotel: Reception scans your passport. Registration is automatic. Keep the registration slip.

Private rental / Airbnb / friend's apartment: You must register at the local police station (派出所) in person.

What to bring to the police station:

Original passport

Lease agreement (or written invitation letter from host)

Host's Chinese ID copy (房东身份证复印件)

Property ownership certificate copy (房产证复印件) — the host must provide this

If you are staying with a Chinese citizen who owns the property: They must accompany you or provide all documents above. Police stations may also require the host to sign a "statement of responsibility" (治安责任书) confirming they are responsible for you during your stay.

Failure to register triggers a fine of RMB 500,000 and may complicate your residence permit application later.


## 3.2 Day 1: Physical Examination at Designated Hospital

You must undergo a health examination at a hospital designated by China Customs (海关). In most major cities, this is the International Travel Health Care Center (国际旅行卫生保健中心).

What to bring:

Original passport

Original physical exam form from your home country (if any)

2 passport photos

Fasting (for blood tests)

What they test:

General physical examination

Chest X-ray (for tuberculosis screening)

Blood tests (HIV, syphilis, hepatitis)

ECG

Timeline: Results take 3 working days. You receive a Health Certificate (健康证明书) — valid for 6 months — which you must submit for your residence permit.

Cost: Approximately RMB 500 depending on the city. Your employer may reimburse this.


## 3.3 Day 5: Work Permit Application (If Not Already Processed)

If your employer did not process the work permit before your arrival (some employers do this after entry), you must apply now.

The process (simplified):

Employer creates an account on the Foreigners Work Management System

Employer uploads all documents:

Your authenticated degree (with Apostille + Chinese translation)

Your authenticated police clearance (with Apostille + Chinese translation)

Your employment contract

Your health certificate

Your passport information page

Digital photo

Approval: 5 working days. You receive an electronic Work Permit Card (or notification).

Physical card: Some cities now issue physical cards; others issue only electronic records. Beijing issues a physical card with a QR code.

Crucial: The work permit is tied to your specific employer and specific job role. If you change employers, you cannot simply "transfer" — you must apply for a new work permit with the new employer, and the process restarts.


## 3.4 Day 15: Residence Permit Application (THE MOST CRITICAL STEP)

This is the step that eliminates risk of illegal stay. Your Z-visa is valid for 30 days from entry. If you fail to obtain the Work-Class Residence Permit within those 30 days, you are overstaying. Overstays trigger daily fines and may lead to visa cancellation and removal from China.

What you need to bring to the Exit & Entry Administration (出入境管理局):


| Document | Detail |
| --- | --- |
| Completed residence permit application form | Available at the bureau or online |
| Original passport | Must have at least 6 months validity |
| Work Permit (notification or physical card) | Proof that you are legally employed |
| Health Certificate | Issued by the designated health center |
| Temporary accommodation registration slip | From the police station or hotel |
| 1 passport-size photos | Chinese photo specifications |
| Employer's official letter | Confirming employment and accommodation |
| Registration fee payment | Typically RMB 800 for 1-year permits |

Processing time: 5 working days. You receive a passport sticker (residence permit) with the validity period.

Length of residence permit: Usually issued for 1 year initially, renewable annually. High-end talent (Category A) may receive 2 year permits.

Critical warning: During this 30-day window, you are physically in China but your legal stay is in a transitional state. Do not travel internationally during this period unless absolutely necessary. Re-entry after travel resets the clock and may cause complications.


# Part IV: Working in China – Employment Rights and Obligations


## 4.1 Your Employment Contract – What Must Be Included

Under Chinese law, your employment contract must be in writing and in Chinese. Key provisions that must be present:

Employer's full legal name and registered address

Your name, passport number, and address

Work location (the actual city and office, not just "China")

Job description (title and main responsibilities)

Start date and, if fixed-term, end date

Compensation (monthly base salary, bonus structure, payment dates)

Working hours and leave entitlement

Social insurance provisions (see Section 4.2)

Termination conditions

If any of these are missing, the contract may be partially void under Chinese law. In case of dispute, courts and labor arbitration tribunals examine contract terms closely.


## 4.2 Social Insurance (五险) and Housing Fund (住房公积金) – Your Legal Entitlement

This is frequently misunderstood by both employers and employees.

Legal basis: Article 97 of the Social Insurance Law of China states: "Foreigners employed within the territory of China shall participate in social insurance in accordance with the provisions of this Law." This is not optional for the employer — it is mandatory.

What you are entitled to:


| Component | Employee Contribution | Employer Contribution | Key Benefit |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Pension (养老保险) | 8% of salary | 16% of salary | Monthly pension after retirement age (if 15+ years paid) |
| Medical (医疗保险) | 2% of salary | ~9% of salary | Hospital reimbursement (varies by city) |
| Unemployment (失业保险) | 0.5% | 0.5% | Benefit if involuntarily unemployed |
| Work Injury (工伤保险) | None | 0.2.9% | Covers workplace injuries |
| Maternity (生育保险) | None | ~0.5% | Maternity leave benefits (if applicable) |
| Housing Fund (住房公积金) | 5% | 5% | Can be withdrawn upon leaving China |

For holders of Permanent Residency (外国永久居留身份证): Courts have confirmed that employers must pay full five social insurances and housing fund for permanent residents in a full-time employment relationship. This obligation applies even in academic/institutional settings.

For non-permanent residents: Contributions are still mandatory. However:

Some bilateral social security agreements (e.g., with Germany, South Korea) allow exemption from certain contributions (typically pension). Check if your home country has such an agreement.

You cannot opt-out "by mutual agreement" — employers who fail to contribute face penalties, and you are entitled to back contributions.

What happens to your contributions when you leave China:

Pension: You can withdraw your personal contributions upon departure. Employer contributions remain in the system.

Housing fund: You can withdraw the full balance (employee + employer contributions) upon leaving China.


## 4.3 Taxes – Individual Income Tax (IIT)

Residency status determines your tax obligations:

Tax resident (resident for 183+ days in a calendar year): Worldwide income taxed in China

Non-tax resident (less than 183 days): Only China-sourced income taxed

Tax brackets (2026):


| Annual Taxable Income (RMB) | Rate |
| --- | --- |
| Up to 36,000 | 3% |
| 36,001,000 | 10% |
| 144,001,000 | 20% |
| 300,001,000 | 25% |
| 420,001,000 | 30% |
| 660,001,000 | 35% |
| Over 960,000 | 45% |

Special deductions: You are entitled to claim:

Basic deduction: RMB 60,000/year

Housing rent: up to RMB 1,500/month (depending on city)

Children's education

Continuing education

Elderly parent care

Action: Your employer handles monthly withholding. You must file an annual reconciliation (汇算清缴) between March-June of the following year — through the state tax app (个人所得税APP) — to claim refunds or pay additional amounts. If you are leaving China mid-year, your final tax settlement should be completed at departure.


## 4.4 Changing Employers – The Full Reset Rule

This is a frequent source of legal problems.

If you change employers in China, you cannot simply "transfer" your work permit.

The process is a full re-application:

Your current employer must cancel your work permit upon your departure

Your new employer must file a new work permit application (same process as initial, using all documents)

Once the new work permit is approved, you apply for a new residence permit (not an extension — a new application)

Critical: Your residence permit remains valid only while you are employed by the original employer. If you resign and the new permit is not yet approved, you may be in a limbo state. Legal practitioners advise:

Do not resign until the new employer's work permit notification is approved

Keep a buffer period between jobs (your existing residence permit remains valid until its expiry date, even if you are not working — but you must not work without the new permit)


# Part V: Termination, Resignation, and Dispute Resolution


## 5.1 Resignation (Quitting)

Notice period: Under Chinese labor law, employees must give 30 days' written notice for resignation. The employer is not required to approve early departure.

Employer's obligations upon your resignation:

Issue release letter (离职证明) — this is legally required

Cancel your work permit (this must be done by the employer)

Issue final salary settlement including unpaid wages, bonuses, and unused annual leave compensation

Critical risk: Some employers refuse to cancel work permits as leverage during disputes. This is illegal — multiple arbitration cases have found that employers who refuse to cancel permits to pressure employees are liable for resulting damages.


## 5.2 Termination by Employer – What You Are Entitled To

With cause termination (serious misconduct, criminal conviction, etc.): No severance.

Without cause termination: You are entitled to statutory severance pay:

One month's salary for each year of service

Partial year counts pro-rata

Illegal termination: If the employer terminates you without legal basis and without following proper procedures, you may be entitled to double the statutory severance (2N).

What to do if you are terminated and disagree with the reason:

Do not sign termination documents under pressure — signing acknowledges agreement

Do not delay action — the statute of limitations for labor disputes is one year


## 5.3 Dispute Resolution Pathways

Chinese labor law provides multiple enforcement mechanisms for foreign employees:


| Pathway | Timeline | Typical Outcome |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Internal negotiation | 1 weeks | Settlement agreement — fastest but requires willing employer |
| Labor arbitration (劳动仲裁) | 30 days | Binding award; can be enforced by courts |
| Litigation (法院诉讼) | 3 months | After arbitration (if appeal); final binding judgment |

Recent cases show that foreign employees win arbitrations regularly. A 2026 case in Xining, Qinghai had a foreign employee successfully recover compensation and severance through arbitration in 7 days — the tribunal used translation tools and bilingual mediation to reach a settlement.

In Beijing's Fangshan District, an arbitration case involving a foreign employee's work permit cancellation was resolved with the employer ultimately providing the required cancellation and compensation.

Practical recommendation:

Document everything — emails, contracts, payslips, WeChat messages with HR or managers

Keep a formal log of all work hours, particularly if your contract specifies overtime rules

If you suspect unfair treatment, consult a labor lawyer early (cost: approximately RMB 2,000,000 for consultation)

Shanghai's legal community now has established labor law specialists focusing on foreign employees


# Part VI: Data Privacy and Cross-Border Data – What Your Employer Cannot Do


## 6.1 The Regulatory Framework

China has enacted the Personal Information Protection Law (PIPL, 个人信息保护法), the Data Security Law (DSL), and the Cybersecurity Law (CSL), which create significant restrictions on how your employer can handle your personal and work data — especially when exporting it overseas.


## 6.2 What Applies to You

Your employer must obtain your consent before:

Transferring your personal data (passport information, health data, performance data) to a parent company or IT system outside China

Processing your biometric data (fingerprints, facial scans)

Sharing your data with third-party vendors outside China

Quantitative thresholds that trigger mandatory government filings:

If your employer exports personal data of 100,000+ individuals (cumulative) in a year, they must file a standard contract with the local cyberspace authority

If your employer exports personal data of 1 million+ individuals (cumulative), they must undergo a full security assessment

What this means for you: Your employer cannot automatically sync your work file, vacation records, performance reviews, and payroll data to a global HR system without your consent and without satisfying these filing requirements.

Recent 2026 practice: The Shanghai government implemented a negative-list system for data exports that significantly reduces the compliance burden for foreign enterprises. Companies in the Shanghai Free Trade Zone and designated pilot areas can now export some operational data under simplified procedures.


## 6.3 Your Rights Under PIPL

Right to access: You can request copies of all personal data held by your employer

Right to deletion: You can request deletion when data is no longer needed for its original purpose

Right to portability: You can request data transfer to another platform (where technically feasible)

Right to object: You can object to processing for direct marketing

These rights are legally enforceable in China.

# Part IX: Common Pitfalls to Avoid

Pitfall 1: "My Employer Said They Would Handle Everything"

Reality: Employers handle the application submission. You must provide the authenticated documents, complete the health exam, attend the police station for registration, and physically go to the Exit & Entry Administration for the residence permit. Your physical presence cannot be delegated. If you fail to show up for the residence permit application, it does not happen.

Pitfall 2: "I Don't Need Social Insurance Because I Won't Retire Here"

Reality: Social insurance is mandatory under Chinese law. Even if you don't intend to claim it, your employer's failure to contribute creates legal exposure for them. More importantly, when you leave China, you can withdraw your personal pension contributions and your full housing fund balance — these are direct cash refunds. Refusing contributions is refusing money that is legally yours.

Pitfall 3: "I Have a Verbal Agreement for Flexible Hours"

Reality: Chinese labor law enforcement emphasizes written contracts and written approvals. Verbal agreements are nearly impossible to enforce in arbitration. If you want flexible hours, overtime pay, or special conditions, get them in writing in the contract or via a formal addendum stamped by the employer.

Pitfall 4: "I'll Sort Out the Police Registration Later"

Reality: The 24-hour accommodation registration is not a suggestion — it is a legal requirement with penalties. It is also a prerequisite for the residence permit. If you don't have the registration slip, you cannot apply for the residence permit, and your Z-visa will expire before you can resolve the issue.
