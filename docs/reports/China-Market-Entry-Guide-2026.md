## Part I: The 2026 Market Reality — Why Preparation Matters

### 1.1 The Opportunity — and the Trap

The numbers that matter:

| Indicator | 2026 Data |
|---|---|
| Total retail sales (H1) | RMB 24.87 trillion (+1.3% YoY) |
| Online retail | RMB 10.07 trillion (+5.2%) |
| Cross-border e-commerce | Growing faster than domestic e-commerce |
| New foreign-invested enterprises (2025, 10 months) | 53,000+ |

The trap: The low entry barrier of cross-border e-commerce (CBEC) creates a false sense of simplicity. "Just list on Tmall Global and start selling." In reality, successful online sales in China do not begin with listing products on a platform. They begin with the right legal structure, compliance framework, and IP strategy-15.

### 1.2 What Changed in 2026 for Foreign Brands

Manufacturing fully open: Since November 2024, manufacturing is fully open to 100% foreign-owned enterprises under the Negative List reform. This removes the most common reason foreign investors historically chose a Joint Venture-14.
E-commerce is now structural, not experimental: China's e-commerce operates through multiple platforms (Tmall, JD, Pinduoduo), each with distinct audiences and requirements. The era of "testing the market" through a single channel is over-1.
Consumer trust is everything: Chinese consumers value credibility, authenticity, and brand heritage. A brand that communicates clearly and consistently, delivers high-quality products, and respects consumer behavior has a better chance of standing out-1.

## Part II: Market Entry Structures — Choose Before You Start

### 2.1 The Four Options

| Structure | Best For | Pros | Cons | Setup Timeline |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| WFOE (Wholly Foreign-Owned Enterprise) | 80% of foreign companies — full operations, invoicing, direct hiring | 100% ownership and control; can invoice in RMB; can hire directly; IP owned by WFOE | Longer setup (8-12 weeks); higher compliance burden | 8-12 weeks |
| Joint Venture (JV) | Negative-List sectors; industries requiring local partner capabilities | Access to restricted sectors; leverage partner's existing infrastructure and channels | Shared profits; less control; IP risks; inheriting partner liabilities | 12-16 weeks |
| Representative Office (RO) | Market exploration phase; liaison; non-revenue presence | Easiest to set up (4-6 weeks); low initial cost | Cannot invoice in RMB; cannot hire directly (must use dispatch agency); limited commercial activities | 4-6 weeks |
| Employer of Record (EOR) | 1-10 hires without establishing an entity | Fast (2 weeks); no entity setup | Limited capabilities; temporary arrangement | 2 weeks |

The 2026 verdict: For 80% of foreign companies, the WFOE is the right answer. For sectors with foreign ownership caps, a Joint Venture may be mandatory. For short-term testing without full commitment, an EOR can provide speed-2-14.

### 2.2 What the Negative List Means for Your Brand

The Negative List (2024 version) is organized into 11 sectors with 29 restricted entries-14.
Sectors that still carry equity caps or prohibitions:
Telecommunications, radio, TV, satellite services
Financial services (some sub-sectors liberalized, others still restricted)
Education (compulsory and religious education prohibited)
Culture, sport, and entertainment (news media, publishing, broadcasting)
Key change: Manufacturing is now fully open. If your business activity sits outside the restricted sectors, a WFOE is almost always the right structure-14.

### 2.3 Registered Capital — Don't Get This Wrong

No minimum since 2014 for most industries. But the 5-year paid-in rule (Article 47 of the 2024 Company Law) means you must transfer the full declared capital into the WFOE's bank account within 5 years-2-14.
Why this matters for your brand:
Setting registered capital too low means the company risks running out of operating funds before generating income
Setting it too high ties up capital that could be better used elsewhere
Regulators use registered capital as a proxy for company size — affecting eligibility for tax incentives, government funding, and project bidding-5
Recommendation: Forecast upfront and ongoing costs to fund operations for approximately one to two years, including premises, equipment, staffing, vendor fees, taxes, and planned expansion-5.

## Part III: Intellectual Property — The Non-Negotiable Priority

### 3.1 The First-to-File Rule — What Every Brand Owner Must Understand

China operates on an entirely different principle from what most Western brands are accustomed to: first to file wins.
Under the Chinese Trademark Law, administered by the China National Intellectual Property Administration (CNIPA), trademark rights belong to whoever files the application first — regardless of whether they created the brand, have ever used it, or have any legitimate connection to the goods or services in question-4.
Real risk: A European manufacturing company trading under its brand name for fifteen years across Europe begins exploring the Chinese market — and discovers its brand name was registered with the CNIPA three years earlier by a completely unrelated party. Under a first-to-file system, that third party may have a stronger legal claim to the mark in China than the original brand owner-4.

### 3.2 The Chinese Name Question — A Separate Registration Entirely

Critical point often overlooked: Registering your brand name in its original Latin-script form does not protect your brand in its Chinese-character version. You must register both separately.
Why this matters:
Chinese consumers will refer to your brand in Chinese characters
A squatter can register your Chinese-brand name even if you own the Latin-script trademark
To build brand recognition in China, you need a consistent, legally protected Chinese brand name
Practical advice: Register your trademarks broadly in both English and Chinese. This is non-negotiable-11. Protect your brand before someone else registers it for you.

### 3.3 The Squatting Risk — Getting Harder to Address

Since early 2025, practitioners have observed a significant tightening of CNIPA's approach to non-use cancellation proceedings — one of the primary tools brand owners use to cancel squatted marks. The evidentiary requirements have been raised considerably, placing a heavier burden on the legitimate rights holder seeking to reclaim a mark. What was previously a relatively accessible remedy has become more complex and costly-4.
Who is at risk:
Any company with brand recognition, distinctive products, or plans to enter the Chinese market
Smaller businesses are particularly vulnerable: professional squatters frequently monitor European trade press, export records, and exhibition registrations-4
One real case: A Portuguese SME with no customers in China was contacted by a Chinese law firm alerting them to an active trademark squatting attempt — simply because their brand had appeared in a sectoral directory-4.

### 3.4 How to Protect Your Brand — The Action Plan

File early. The moment you decide on your brand name and logo for the Chinese market, the clock starts ticking. Filing for trademark registration in the relevant classes (often more than you initially think) should run in parallel with your company registration, not after-9.
File broadly. Register in all relevant categories. A too-narrow scope can legally bar future revenue streams and constrain your brand narrative-9.
File defensively. If you discover an unauthorized filing, use the opposition period (3 months from publication) to block it. One international brand successfully opposed an unauthorized filing in 2022, and by 2024, its trademarks were officially registered, securing exclusive rights in China for the following ten years-8.
Persist through setbacks. A rejection is not final. Appeals and re-filings can ultimately secure protection. The CNIPA's Review and Adjudication Department handles refusal reviews; if the review fails, appeal to the Beijing IP Court-13.

## Part IV: Choosing Your Market Entry Channel

### 4.1 Cross-Border E-Commerce (CBEC) — The Low-Barrier Option

How it works: Foreign companies can sell into China without establishing a local entity through designated CBEC platforms.
What you need to check BEFORE launching:

| Checkpoint | Detail |
|---|---|
| Product category | Is your product on the CBEC "positive list"? Not all products qualify |
| Customs registration | Who will handle customs registration and act as the responsible party vis-à-vis Chinese customs authorities? |
| Labels and documentation | Have product labels, descriptions, and documentation been adapted to Chinese requirements? |
| Transaction limits | CBEC has per-transaction and per-person annual limits |

The tradeoff: CBEC offers lower entry barriers and a faster market test — but you face product category restrictions, transaction limits, platform requirements, and limitations regarding customer data access-15.

### 4.2 Platform Selection — Which Marketplace Fits Your Brand

| Platform | Audience | Entry Rules | Best-Fit SKUs | Not a Fit If… |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Tmall Global | Prestige buyers | High deposits + annual fees + commissions | Premium SKUs, proven brands with margin | Brand is untested; margins are thin |
| JD Worldwide | Quality-driven buyers | Compliance + setup costs | Higher ASP, spec-heavy goods needing assurance | Low-price SKUs, no trust signals |
| Taobao | Everyday consumers | Local entity or partner required | Wide range, fast-moving consumer goods | No local entity; no distributor |
| Pinduoduo | Bargain hunters | Low barriers, thin margins | Accessories, bundles, overstock | Core SKUs that carry brand reputation |

### 4.3 The Distribution Model — Control vs. Speed

Direct distribution (via WFOE):
Full control over pricing, branding, customer relationships, and sales channels
Higher costs, longer preparation, extensive compliance obligations
You own the customer data and can build a direct relationship
Distributor model:
Simpler market entry
Reduced control over pricing, branding, customer relationships, and customer data-15
Critical due diligence for distributors:

| Assessment Area | What to Check |
|---|---|
| Market experience | Sales channels, customer resources, market promotion capability |
| Financial status | Sufficient funds to purchase and sell products |
| Legal compliance | Tax laws, labor laws, consumer protection compliance |
| Reputation | Business reputation and market standing |

The contractual trap: When establishing a distribution relationship, both parties tend to focus on commercial terms and overlook clauses for termination. If these clauses are missing or vague, the brand owner may find itself at a disadvantage in case of disputes — affecting the brand's subsequent business arrangements and development-10.
Key provisions your distribution agreement MUST include:
Clear scope of authorization: Define distribution area, sales channels, exclusivity, and product range. Separate online and offline channels-10.
Brand protection clauses: Grant distributors authorization to report infringements on online platforms so platforms can take quick action — delisting infringing products and links-10.
Ownership of intangible assets: Clarify who owns market information, customer data, and sales data accumulated during the cooperation. Brand flagship stores are usually registered under the distributor's company, not the brand owner's-10.
Termination provisions: Address what happens to the flagship store account, remaining inventory, and customer relationships after termination. Without these clauses, the original distributor may sell the brand's products at a low price, distorting the market and confusing consumers-10.

## Part V: Operational Compliance — What You Must Do

### 5.1 Product Labelling and Import Requirements

Chinese labelling requirement: Labels must contain mandatory Chinese-language information, including:
Product name
Model specifications
Applicable standards
Manufacturer and importer information
Relevant warnings
Consequence of errors: Customs delays, rejection of goods, or platform-related issues-15.
HS Code classification: The HS Code determines not only applicable duties and taxes but also whether additional requirements such as registrations, certifications, or approvals apply. In many cases, a local agent is also required-15.

### 5.2 Marketing Claims — What You Cannot Say

Chinese sales and marketing rules are detailed and strictly enforced-11. Exaggerated statements, absolute claims ("best," "number one"), or unauthorized health-related claims can create significant legal risks-15.
What to check:
Product descriptions must be accurate and complete
Claims must be supported by verifiable evidence
Health-related claims require regulatory approval

### 5.3 Data Protection — The PIPL Impact

If your e-commerce operations generate customer, payment, logistics, and support data that is transferred to Europe or integrated into global systems, additional regulatory requirements apply under the Personal Information Protection Law-15.
What this means for your brand:
Data collected in China must generally be stored on local servers
Cross-border data transfers require compliance mechanisms (standard contracts or security assessments)
Customer consent must be obtained for data processing
Practical implication: Your global CRM system cannot automatically sync Chinese customer data without satisfying these requirements.

## Part VI: Building Your Brand in China — Beyond the Legal Framework

### 6.1 Localization — Not Just Translation

China's market is not one single homogenous market. It's dozens of markets — online, offline, different e-commerce ecosystems, and different by geography-11.
What localization requires in 2026:
Digital infrastructure: Localizing domain names, websites, and other digital assets. This involves both translating assets into Chinese and localizing them to fit local preferences for design and user experience-5.
ERP/IT localization: Global ERPs must be localized for China or replaced with separate systems to comply with data regulations and generate financial reports adhering to Chinese GAAP-5.
WeChat presence: To open an official branded WeChat subscription account (essential for brand building), a WFOE or JV is typically a prerequisite-9.

### 6.2 The Registered Address — More Than a Formality

Your company's registered address is not merely a line on a form; it's a geographic anchor for your brand identity-9.
Brand signaling:
A prestigious location (Lujiazui, the Bund) signals stability, success, and global connectivity — important for B2B and professional service firms
An industrial park location signals manufacturing or cost-effectiveness — aligning with an industrial brand
The risk: Using an invalid address can lead to deregistration. In one case, a startup using a cut-rate virtual address from a non-compliant provider was flagged for "abnormal operation" status on the public credit system — a severe blow to their nascent brand credibility-9.

### 6.3 The 3-5 Year Horizon

The most successful international brands in China think in 3-5 year horizons. They invest in compliance, credible import partners, consistent market presence, and then they scale. Trust before volume. Every time-11.

## Part VII: Common Pitfalls — Case Studies

Pitfall 1: "My EU Trademark Protects Me in China"
Reality: An EU trademark registered at the EUIPO provides zero protection in China. The two systems are entirely independent. A company that has diligently protected its brand across Europe may have no rights whatsoever in China if it has not filed with the CNIPA-4.
Fix: File with CNIPA before your brand becomes visible in any international context — trade press, export records, exhibition registrations.
Pitfall 2: "I'll Use a Local Distributor and Retain Control"
Reality: If the distribution agreement doesn't address ownership of customer data, online store accounts, and termination procedures, the distributor may own your brand's flagship store account. After termination, the original distributor can continue selling your products at low prices, distorting the market and confusing consumers-10.
Fix: In the distribution agreement, clarify who owns the flagship store account, customer data, and sales data. Include termination provisions that address account transfer and inventory buyback.
Pitfall 3: "I'll Register My Brand Name First, Figure Out the Chinese Name Later"
Reality: A squatter can register your Chinese-brand name separately from your Latin-script trademark. You need to protect both versions.
Fix: File both the Latin-script and Chinese-character versions simultaneously.
Pitfall 4: "I'll Start with Cross-Border E-Commerce — It's Simple"
Reality: CBEC requires checking product eligibility against the positive list, customs registration, labelling compliance, and understanding platform requirements. What initially seems like a simple market entry quickly becomes a legal and compliance project-15.
Fix: Before launching, confirm product category eligibility, identify who will handle customs registration, and ensure labels and documentation are fully compliant.
