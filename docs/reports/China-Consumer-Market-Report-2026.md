# China Consumer Market Report - 2026 Edition

## Foreword

You have seen the headline numbers. China's total retail sales reached RMB 24.87 trillion in H1 2026, growing 1.3% year-on-year. Online retail hit RMB 10.07 trillion, up 5.2%. Service retail grew 5.3% — more than quadruple the pace of goods retail.
But these headlines obscure the structural shifts that define what it actually means to sell into China in 2026. This is not a market of uniform growth. It is a market of divergence — between tiers of cities, between age cohorts, between retail formats, and between domestic and foreign brands. Understanding these divergences is the difference between allocating resources effectively and watching them disappear.
This is not a superficial summary. This is the strategic analysis that covers: the true state of consumer confidence and spending power, the demographic transformation that is reshaping demand, why Tier 4-5 cities are now the primary growth engines, the channel dynamics where e-commerce dominates and O2O is rebounding, the categories where consumption is growing and where it is contracting, the value-seeking behavior that now defines consumer decision-making, and what all of this means for brands and retailers operating in China.

### 1.3 Consumer Confidence — The Income-Spending Gap

Per capita disposable income grew 4.2% in real terms. Per capita consumption expenditure grew only 2.7%. 
This 1.5 percentage point gap is the most important data point in this report. Chinese consumers are earning more but spending less of what they earn. The precautionary savings motive remains elevated. This is not a crisis — but it is a structural constraint on consumption growth that brands must account for.

### 2.2 The Contrarian Interpretation

The consensus narrative celebrates service consumption growth as evidence of healthy structural upgrading. There is another interpretation: **consumers may be shifting spending from goods to services not because they prefer experiences, but because goods consumption is being suppressed by weak confidence in durable goods purchases. **
Signal to watch: Durable goods categories (home appliances, furniture, automobiles) over the next 2-3 months. If these stabilize or improve, the cyclical interpretation gains weight. If they continue to decline, the structural shift is likely permanent.

## Part III: The Demographic Transformation

### 3.1 The Numbers That Matter

China's consumer market is being reshaped by two demographic forces: |Demographic Indicator|2026 Figure| |---|---| |Population aged 60+|~320 million| |Single-person households|~25% of all households| These are not marginal trends. They are structural shifts that alter what consumers buy and how they buy it.
Implications for brands:
Aging population: Increased demand for health-related products, convenient packaging, smaller portion sizes, accessible retail formats
Single-person households: More frequent, smaller shopping trips; demand for ready-to-eat and single-serve products; higher willingness to pay for convenience

### 3.2 The Age Effect on Spending

Older households in lower-tier cities recorded faster FMCG spending growth than younger households in Tier 1 and 2 cities. Households with children in Tier 5 cities also stood out, maintaining relatively high consumption intensity, particularly in categories tied to childcare and daily necessities.

## Part IV: The Geography of Growth — Tier 4-5 Cities

### 4.1 The Growth Shift

Tier 1 cities: Recorded low or slightly negative FMCG value growth in 2025.
Tier 4 and 5 cities: Generated a disproportionate share of incremental FMCG value.
This is a fundamental reorientation of where consumption growth is happening. The engines of incremental demand are now in lower-tier markets, not in Beijing, Shanghai, Guangzhou, and Shenzhen.

### 4.2 Drivers of Lower-Tier Growth

Ongoing urbanization: Rural-to-urban migration continues in these regions
Improved logistics infrastructure: Modern retail and digital channels reaching further
Expanded product availability: Broader assortments in more locations
Rising incomes: Still growing faster than in saturated Tier 1 markets

### 4.3 What This Means for Brands

If your brand's distribution and marketing strategy is Tier 1-heavy, you are competing in the slowest-growing part of the market. The growth opportunity is in lower-tier cities — but success there requires different product positioning, pricing, and channel strategies. Value-for-money is paramount, but consumers are not simply choosing the lowest price — they are evaluating trade-offs between price, quality, pack size, and convenience.

## Part V: Channel Dynamics — Where Consumers Are Shopping

### 5.1 E-Commerce Dominance

E-commerce accounted for 38% of urban FMCG sales in 2025.
The 38% figure understates the strategic significance of e-commerce. Digital channels are not just a channel — they are increasingly becoming the primary point of discovery and purchase for a wide range of categories.

### 5.2 O2O — The Turning Point

Online-to-offline (O2O) FMCG sales grew nearly 8% year-on-year in Q3 2025.
After two volatile years, O2O has reached a clear turning point. Drivers include:
Faster delivery networks
Platform-led promotional coordination
Expansion beyond fresh food into broader grocery and household categories

### 5.3 Offline Winners

### 5.5 Private Labels — A Rising Force

Private labels accounted for approximately 2% of total FMCG sales in urban China in 2025 — but private-label FMCG spending grew over 57% year-on-year, reaching RMB 32.7 billion.
This is a signal that private labels are becoming an increasingly important strategic lever for retailers. They make value more visible to consumers and build differentiated propositions.

## Part VII: The Value-Seeking Consumer

### 7.1 The "Trading Down" Dynamic

In 2025, total urban FMCG spending rose just 0.9% year-on-year — as a 3.6% increase in volumes was offset by a 2.6% decline in average selling prices.
What this means: Consumers are buying more units but paying less per unit. This is not a recession — it is a structural shift in consumer behavior. The "value for money" mindset has become dominant.

### 7.2 What "Value for Money" Actually Means

Rather than simply opting for the lowest price, households are increasingly evaluating trade-offs between:
Price and quality
Price and pack size
Price and channel convenience
Consumers are managing household budgets through a combination of product, store, and channel choices — not simply defaulting to the cheapest option.

### 7.3 Domestic Brands Gaining Share

Domestic brands strengthened their market position across multiple categories in 2025, benefiting from:
Local consumer insights
Rapid product innovation
Fast innovation cycles
Competitive pricing
Foreign brands that succeed are those that can combine global brand equity with local execution at speed.

### 8.2 The 15th Five-Year Consumption Plan

The State Council issued the first-ever five-year plan dedicated to consumption in July 2026.
Key target by 2030: Total retail sales to reach approximately RMB 60 trillion (up from RMB 50.1 trillion in 2025).
28 specific measures across 6 areas, including:
Financial support mechanisms
Consumer loan subsidies
Tax rebate programs
Lottery incentive pilots

### 8.3 The Countervailing View

If the trade-in program is generating 1:10.3 leverage, this suggests policy is successfully stimulating goods replacement — but this may be pulling forward demand that would otherwise occur naturally. The sustainability of goods consumption without policy support is unproven.
