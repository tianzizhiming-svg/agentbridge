Foreword

You have arrived in China. Your phone is now a very expensive clock.

Google Maps is blocked. WhatsApp is gone. You cannot call Uber, you cannot pay at restaurants, and hotel clerks stare because you are waving a credit card that does not work here.

This is not a travel guide about the Great Wall or the Forbidden City. You can find those anywhere. This is the manual that 47 million foreign travelers wish they had before boarding their flight. No politics. No fluff. Just the hard-core digital toolkit you need to operate in a country where everything runs on apps you have never heard of—and where your usual digital ecosystem is completely absent.

China lives inside smartphones. Cash is practically dead. International credit cards are not accepted at local stalls. If you land thinking you can "wing it" with Google, Uber, and your physical credit card, you are going to pay a massive "ignorance tax" in every taxi, restaurant, and souvenir shop.

This guide will make sure you don't.

Part I: What You're Walking Into

The Parallel Digital Universe

China runs on its own internet ecosystem—a parallel digital universe that is genuinely impressive once you get the hang of it. But if you arrive unprepared, the Great Firewall will quietly block most of the apps that keep your life together.

Here is what gets cut off the moment you land:

The trap: Most tourists Google "best VPN for China" from their hotel room on day one. Too late. The app stores are already geo-restricted.

The move: Set up everything before you board the plane. Everything.

Complete these tasks before you leave home:

  • Set up Alipay with international card (International version)
  • Set up WeChat Pay (optional but recommended)

  • Pack plug adapter (types A, C, I)

Part III: The VPN Situation — Get This Right First

Not all VPNs work in China, and the ones that do have varying reliability. As of 2026, names with decent track records include ExpressVPN, Astrill, and NordVPN — though China's firewall updates regularly, so nothing is guaranteed.

What to Do

  • Download your VPN at home, not in China
  • Set up and test the connection before departure
  • Save offline maps on Google Maps before you fly (or switch to Maps.me, which works without data)
Pro tip: Astrill tends to be the most consistently reliable for mainland China, but it is pricier. If budget matters, grab ExpressVPN and keep Astrill as backup. Pay for two. You will thank yourself later.

Plan B: International SIMs/eSIMs like Airalo or your home carrier's roaming package give you data that bypasses Chinese restrictions — meaning you get your regular internet, no VPN needed for data. The tradeoff: it is often slower and more expensive per GB.

Part IV: Payment — The Number One Survival Skill

The Reality

Cash is practically dead in China's major cities. International credit cards are rarely accepted at local vendors, street stalls, taxis, or small restaurants. This is the single most important setup step for your trip.

Alipay (Primary Choice)

Since 2023, Alipay has become significantly more accessible for foreigners. You can now link an international Visa or Mastercard directly to Alipay — no Chinese bank account required.

Step-by-step setup:

  1. Download Alipay before arriving
  2. Open the app and select the "International" option
  3. Link your foreign credit card (Visa/Mastercard)
  4. Complete identity verification (passport details)
  5. You are ready to pay

WeChat Pay (Backup Option)

WeChat Pay has also opened up to international cards, though the setup is slightly more involved through the WeChat app itself.

The 3% Transaction Fee Rule

When you link a foreign Visa or Mastercard to Alipay or WeChat Pay, a 3% transaction fee applies to each payment. This fee is charged on top of the purchase amount and is in addition to any foreign transaction fee your home bank may charge.

What this means in practice: A ¥100 meal costs you ¥103 via Alipay with a foreign card. For small daily purchases this is negligible, but for large hotel bills or shopping it adds up quickly.

How to minimize it: If you are staying more than a week, consider loading a prepaid CNY-denominated card through your bank before departure, or using cash for larger transactions where the fee becomes significant.

Government-Backed Guidance

In 2026, the China Payment and Clearing Association (PCAC) published the "Payment Guide for Foreigners in China," covering bank cards, mobile payments, cash, accounts, and digital RMB. This reflects a nationwide push to improve payment accessibility for international visitors.

The Chinese government has made improving digital payment convenience for foreigners a policy priority. A 2026 directive from 11 national ministries explicitly calls for:

  • Supporting more overseas e-wallets for use in China
  • Expanding cashless payment options for international visitors
  • Enabling more foreign banks and payment platforms to connect with Chinese systems
  • Streamlining passport verification processes

Part V: Connectivity — SIM Cards vs. eSIM

Option 1: International SIM or eSIM

Services like Airalo or your home carrier's roaming package give you data that bypasses Chinese restrictions — meaning you get your regular internet, no VPN needed for data. The tradeoff: slower and more expensive per GB.

Option 2: Local Chinese SIM

Available at the airport and at China Unicom / China Mobile shops, a local SIM gives you fast, cheap data. But everything runs through China's network, meaning you still need your VPN to access blocked apps.

Foreigners can bring passports or Foreign Permanent Resident ID Cards to service offices of telecom operators (China Telecom, China Mobile, China Unicom) to apply for a SIM card and activate mobile communication services in China.

Option 3: Pocket WiFi Rental

Available at Pudong and Hongqiao airports. Good for groups. Slightly annoying to carry everywhere.

Recommendation

For most tourists, Airalo eSIM + a reliable VPN combo is the cleanest setup. Fast enough, flexible, and you are not dependent on finding a SIM shop while jet-lagged at 6am.

DiDi: The Ride-Hailing Lifeboat

Never hail a traditional taxi on the street in tourist zones — they will notice you are a foreigner and charge you double. Use DiDi instead.

DiDi Setup — Critical Details

The SMS Problem: The standalone DiDi app requires SMS verification during registration. If your foreign SIM does not have international SMS roaming enabled in China, you will not receive the OTP code — leaving you stranded at the airport.

Zero-SMS Bypass (Recommended): Open Alipay, search for "DiDi" in the top search bar, and launch the DiDi mini-program. Because your identity and foreign card are already linked to Alipay, it bypasses the need for a separate DiDi registration or SMS verification entirely.

If Using the Standalone DiDi App: Register and verify your phone number at home before departure, or confirm your foreign carrier has incoming SMS roaming enabled for China.

The "Last 4 Digits" Rule: When you get in the car, the driver will almost always ask for a 4-digit code. This is the last 4 digits of the phone number registered on your DiDi account — used to confirm you are the correct passenger before the trip starts.

Taobao: The Ultimate Pricing Hack

See a beautiful "regional exclusive" souvenir in a historic town for $8 USD? Open Taobao, use the visual photo-search camera tool, and watch the exact same official factory item pop up online for $2 USD.

WeChat: More Than Messaging

WeChat is China's everything app — messaging, payments, maps, mini-programs, and food delivery all in one place.

Part VII: Residence Registration — The Legal Requirement

This is not optional. Failure to register can result in fines.

If You Stay in a Hotel

Residence registration is handled by the hotel. Present a valid passport or other international travel documents to reception upon check-in.

If You Stay Elsewhere (Airbnb, Friends, Rental)

You must register at the local police station (Pàichūsuǒ 派出所) within 24 hours of arrival at that address.

What to Bring to the Police Station

  • Passport: Original + copy of photo page + copy of valid visa/entry stamp page
  • Accommodation Proof: Printed Airbnb confirmation, lease contract, or landlord's property certificate copy (Fángchǎn zhèng 房产证)
  • Landlord's Info: Copy of landlord's Chinese ID (Shēnfèn zhèng 身份证) and contact phone number

Online Self-Registration Option

Major tier-1 cities (Shanghai, Beijing, Shenzhen, Guangzhou) now offer online foreign residence registration via web portals or WeChat mini-programs. Check with your landlord or local expat groups to see if you can upload photos of your passport and lease without visiting the station in person.

Part VIII: Visa-Free Transit (144-Hour Policy)

China's 144-hour visa-free transit policy allows citizens of certain countries to stay in designated cities for up to 6 days without a visa — provided they are transiting to a third country or region. This requires precise logistical execution. Key areas to plan:

  • Mandatory legal frameworks to ensure compliance and prevent border rejections
  • Digital survival strategies for configuring Alipay, WeChat Pay, eSIMs, and VPNs before arrival
  • Optimized fast-track itineraries for Beijing, Shanghai, and Chengdu
  • High-speed rail transfers, subway navigation, and ride-hailing protocols

As of November 2024, China expanded visa-free entry to ordinary passport holders from Bulgaria, Romania, Malta, Croatia, Montenegro, North Macedonia, Estonia, Latvia, and Japan on a trial basis. China also extended visa-free stays from 15 days to 30 days and added "exchange visit" as a qualifying purpose. Currently, 38 countries participate in this visa-free arrangement.

Visa-free policies change frequently. Always verify the current list of eligible countries and conditions through official Chinese embassy or consulate websites before travel.

Part X: Official Government Policy Context

The Digital Services Initiative

In 2026, China's Cyberspace Administration (CAC) and 10 other ministries jointly issued the "Implementation Opinions on Improving Digital Service Convenience for Foreigners Entering China." This policy aims to create an interconnected, inclusive, and mutually recognized digital service system by 2027.

By 2027: Digital basic services are smoother and more efficient; payment, living, medical, tourism, and public service digital application bottlenecks are largely resolved; application scenarios comprehensively expanded.

By 2030: Digital services for inbound travelers reach internationally leading levels; full-scenario digital services deeply aligned with international models.

Key Policy Areas

  • Digital Basic Services: Streamlined telecom handling, more foreign-language digital services, comprehensive digital service platforms
  • Digital Payment Services: Supporting more overseas e-wallets in China, expanded deposit-free policies
  • Digital Tourism Services: Online reservations at museums and scenic spots using foreign ID documents, multilingual transportation apps, simplified online hotel registration
  • Digital Public Services: International medical service platforms, online investment and business services, integrated government services ("one-stop online processing")

Part XI: Working and Living in China

Work Permit Integration

Since December 1, 2024, China has integrated the Foreigner Work Permit with the Social Security Card. Foreign workers can now apply for an electronic Social Security Card (e-SSC) following the official guide.

Digital Services in Cities

Shanghai: The city has gone almost entirely cashless. Alipay for foreigners has become much more accessible since 2023. You can now link an international Visa or Mastercard directly to Alipay — no Chinese bank account required.

Guangzhou: Guangzhou's cyberspace office has implemented policies to improve digital services for inbound travelers, focusing on telecommunications, payments, tourism, and public services.

Part XV: The Human Element

Here is the secret nobody puts in a digital survival guide: the best parts of China do not need an app.

The French Concession at golden hour. The chaos of Yu Garden on a weekday morning. The locals doing tai chi along the Bund before sunrise. You cannot Yelp your way into those moments.

The most useful thing you can do — after sorting your VPN and Alipay — is put the phone down and wander. China rewards the people who get lost in it.

China is one of the most exciting countries on the planet. Do not let a Wi-Fi wall be the reason you spend your first afternoon staring at a loading screen. Get the setup right, then go actually see the place.
